# Literary Review: Chapter 8, "Decision-Making Under Uncertainty: How the Brain Chooses When It Cannot Be Sure"

Source: `docs/book/Word/Chapter8/How_the_Brain_Learns_Chapter_8_Decision_Making.md` (474 lines; body runs from line 10 to line 138, about 5,200 words of prose body excluding headings, captions, and footnote text; 7 figures; 76 footnotes). Line numbers refer to the current file.

## Overall assessment

What works: the handover from Chapter 7 is clean. Chapter 7 closes on "How those signals are combined, weighed against uncertainty, and turned into a decision is the subject of Chapter 8," and this chapter opens at the same card table with a sharp sentence, "Whatever choosing well requires, it is not the ability to state the rules" (line 12). The thesis (line 16) is stated plainly and the chapter delivers each of its four claims. The voice matches Chapters 4 to 7: one named experiment and one number per claim, periodic sentences, and contested findings labeled as contested in a sentence each, with no hedging adverbs (Hayden and Niv against Levy and Glimcher at line 26, Latimer and Katz at line 54, Gal and Rucker at line 70, Watts, Duncan, and Quan at line 82, de Wit at line 100). Several passages are as good as anything in the book: the worked speed-accuracy numbers drawn from Figure 8.3 (line 52), Wald and Turing (line 48), the Asian disease problem (line 68), S.M. losing her loss aversion (line 70), Kidd's broken promises and "taking the marshmallow is the rational choice" (line 82), the Breland raccoon (line 104), and "accurate and paralyzed" (line 108). The callbacks to earlier chapters are accurate (I checked lateral inhibition and the subthalamic nucleus in Chapter 4, Miyazaki's waiting rats and unexpected uncertainty in Chapter 3, and the heartbeat-counting caveat and the anterior insula in Chapter 7). American spelling is clean in the body, and there are no em dashes.

What does not work: there are four structural problems. (1) The section order is broken. "Learning What Options Are Worth" (lines 30 to 36) uses LIP, Newsome, the motion task, and the diffusion model before "Accumulating Evidence" (lines 38 to 54) introduces any of them, and line 36 calls accumulation "the accumulation of the next section". The thesis (line 16) and Figure 8.1 both run evidence first, then value, so moving the two evidence sections forward fixes everything. (2) The back third thins out into a sequence of one- and two-paragraph sections ("Knowing How Sure You Are", "Thinking Is Costly", "Choosing Among Others", "When Choice Goes Wrong", each about 190 to 220 words). Two of them read as name catalogues rather than stories, so the momentum of the first half is lost just when the chapter should be building to its lesson. (3) The thesis never mentions learning, in a book called *How the Brain Learns*, and the Central Lesson recaps the sections instead of stating one lesson. (4) The chapter never hands over to Chapter 9 ("Habits, Skills, and Automaticity"), even though its own "Goals and Habits" section is the obvious bridge. The AI section also contains one claim that is out of date for a 2026 book (line 126). Prose quality: 4/5. Reader interest: 4/5. The fixes below are mostly reorderings and targeted expansions, and with them both scores should reach 5.

## Findings

### Major (must fix before publication)

- **Lines 30 to 54 (section order).** "Leo Sugrue, Greg Corrado, and Newsome trained monkeys ... and found that LIP neurons tracked each option's share" (line 32); "just as a motion decision takes longer the weaker the motion" (line 36); "a diffusion model fitted these choices" (line 36); "the accumulation of the next section applied to worth rather than to motion" (line 36). Newsome appears by surname only, LIP is not defined until line 44, the dots task is not introduced until line 40, and the diffusion model is not introduced until line 48. The reader meets four undefined terms in two paragraphs. The order also contradicts the thesis ("It accumulates evidence until it is sure enough ... It represents the value of options", line 16) and Figure 8.1, which runs left to right from evidence to value. **Fix:** move "Accumulating Evidence" (lines 38 to 54) and "Knowing How Sure You Are" (lines 56 to 60) to follow "The Problem Decision Solves", so that the order is: Problem, Accumulating Evidence, Knowing How Sure You Are, A Common Currency, Learning What Options Are Worth, Risk, Loss, and Ambiguity, and so on. Then at line 36 change "the accumulation of the next section applied to worth" to "the accumulation of the dots task applied to worth". Line 14 already opens with "whether the dots on the screen are drifting left or right", so the reader goes from that teaser straight to the dots. Line 24's "These are the regions whose damage, in Chapter 7, left choice impaired" still works, and so does line 34's tie back to the Iowa task.

- **Line 34 (repetition of Chapter 7).** "This is the difficulty that Lesley Fellows and Martha Farah found at the heart of the Iowa gambling task of Chapter 7. ... when the order of the cards was changed so that the bad decks showed their losses early, the patients' deficit largely disappeared." Chapter 7 (its line 168) has already told this result in nearly the same words ("when the early run of large rewards was removed their deficit shrank"). Told twice, it reads like a draft that forgot the previous chapter. Keep it, but as a payoff rather than a retelling: "Chapter 7 left the Iowa patients with a problem of reversal: the bad decks pay well at first and turn bad later, and Fellows and Farah found that when the losses came early the patients' deficit largely disappeared.[^11] The reason is now visible. A learned value is an average over a history, and it changes slowly when the world changes quickly. The region that links value to the body is also the region that lets a learned value be revised." This also cuts about 30 words.

- **Lines 16 and 138 (learning is missing from the thesis; the Central Lesson is a recap).** The thesis at line 16 lists accumulation, valuation, exploration, and multiple controllers, and never mentions learning, so the book's argument is not visible in the chapter's argument. Yet the chapter's best material is about learning: values learned from prediction errors (line 32), reversal (line 34), learning rate and volatility (line 90), habits forming with practice (line 102), and addiction as a corruption of the learning rule (line 120). The Central Lesson (line 138) then summarizes the sections in order ("It values options ... discounts the future ... balances ... runs a flexible planner") rather than stating one lesson, as Chapter 7's does ("The brain's first job is not to know the world but to keep a body alive in it"). **Fix at line 16**, add after "according to which is more reliable": "Every one of these mechanisms is tuned by learning: the values, the bounds, the rate of exploration, and the balance between planning and habit are all set by outcomes." **Rewrite of line 138:** "A decision is a bet placed before the evidence is complete, and the brain is built to place it well rather than to place it perfectly. It accumulates evidence until being wrong costs less than waiting, prices options on a scale set by the state of the body and the options on offer, weights losses and ambiguity more heavily than a calculator would, and keeps paying to learn about options it is not choosing. It runs a planner and a habit system side by side, under the pull of older Pavlovian reflexes, and lets reliability decide which acts. Every one of these settings is learned from outcomes. The Iowa patients could state the rules and still lose because the part of the brain that learns what options are worth, and revises that learning when the world changes, was gone. Choosing well is not knowing the rules; it is a chooser that knows how sure it is, spends thought where thought pays, and learns from every outcome." This also closes the question the chapter opens with at line 12 ("This chapter asks what it is"), which the current lesson never answers.

- **Lines 102 to 120, and the end of the body (no handover to Chapter 9).** Chapter 7 ended its body with a section that handed over a problem. This chapter's body ends on computational psychiatry (line 120), and the Next Chapter line (line 142), "Habits, Skills, and Automaticity: How Practice Turns Choices into Routines", arrives without preparation. "Goals and Habits" also risks taking over Chapter 9's material unless it says where the story continues. **Fix:** end "Goals and Habits" at line 102 (after the Gillan sentence, or after the Pavlovian paragraph at line 104) with one sentence: "How a choice repeated often enough stops being a choice at all, and how the brain turns deliberate action into routine and skill, is the subject of Chapter 9." Then add a closing sentence to "When Choice Goes Wrong" (line 120) that returns to habit: "In each case, what fails is not reasoning but the machinery that learns what to choose, and the most persistent of those failures are habits that have outlived their reasons." The final body section then leads into Chapter 9 as Chapter 7's led into this one.

- **Lines 106 to 120 (the back third is thin).** "Thinking Is Costly" (about 220 words), "Choosing Among Others" (about 190 words, a single paragraph), and "When Choice Goes Wrong" (about 210 words) are each a third the length of "Accumulating Evidence" (810 words). Line 108 is a catalogue: Simon "proposed", Gigerenzer and Goldstein "showed", Lieder and Griffiths "have proposed", Shenhav, Botvinick, and Cohen "proposed", four names in four sentences with no experiment the reader can picture. Line 114 is the only paragraph of its section and ends on a large claim ("felt in the body and learned in a culture") that the chapter does not develop. See "Length and balance" below for the specific expansions (about 400 to 500 words in total). The most important single addition is one concrete heuristic experiment in line 108. For example, Goldstein and Gigerenzer's finding that German students, who had heard of San Diego but not San Antonio, picked the larger city more often than American students who knew both (Goldstein & Gigerenzer 2002, *Psychological Review*). Have the expert reviewer confirm the figures before they go in.

- **Line 126 (AI claim out of date).** "Most deployed models spend the same computation on every input, easy or hard." By 2026 the most visible deployed systems, language models that reason before answering, spend more computation on harder problems, and work on adaptive computation in networks goes back at least to Alex Graves's adaptive computation time (2016). A reader who uses these systems will stop trusting the section at this sentence. The paragraph also has no citation and no named example, unlike points two and four. **Rewrite:** "First, accumulation to a bound is a principle artificial systems have only recently begun to use. Most networks still spend the same computation on every input, easy or hard. Language models that reason step by step before answering spend more on harder problems, which is a step toward the brain's policy, but they rarely have an explicit bound that moves with the stakes or a principled rule for when to stop. The brain spends time in proportion to difficulty, stops when it is sure enough, and raises the bound when errors are expensive." Cite Graves (2016) or a survey of test-time computation if the expert reviewer approves.

### Minor (should fix)

- **Line 52 (paragraph of 237 words).** It covers speed against accuracy, the worked numbers, theta and the subthalamic nucleus, and urgency. Split before "The brain also moves the bound when the choice is hard." In the second half, "a slow theta rhythm over the medial frontal cortex" and "deep brain stimulation" are used without definition in this chapter. Rewrite: "When options conflict, an electrical rhythm of four to eight cycles a second, the theta rhythm, rises over the medial frontal cortex and decisions slow down. James Cavanagh, Michael Frank, and colleagues studied patients with Parkinson's disease treated by deep brain stimulation, in which an implanted electrode delivers continuous pulses to the subthalamic nucleus, the station of the basal ganglia that Chapter 4 described as able to stop everything at once. With the stimulator on, the slowing disappeared and the patients responded impulsively."

- **Lines 24, 32, 48, 70, 76, 90, 102, 114 (paragraph length).** Eight further paragraphs run between 150 and 190 words. Chapter 7's review set about 165 words as the point to consider a split. Split at these points:
  - Line 102: before "In a two-step task designed to separate the systems".
  - Line 114: before "Joseph Henrich and colleagues ran the game" (and see the expansion under "Length and balance").
  - Line 70: before "The size, and for some kinds of choice the existence".
  - Line 24: before "In people, activity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex".
  - Lines 32, 48, 76, and 90 can stand.

- **Line 36.** "recorded the brain's activity during such choices with magnetoencephalography" The term is not defined. Use "with magnetoencephalography, which records the tiny magnetic fields produced by cortical currents from outside the head,".

- **Line 60.** "Confidence also keeps the decision open." The Resulaj experiment that follows is about changes of mind driven by late evidence, not about confidence, so the topic sentence promises something the paragraph does not deliver. Rewrite: "A decision, once committed, also stays open. In the model a choice is committed when a bound is crossed, but evidence already in the pipeline continues to arrive, and Arbora Resulaj ..." The confidence thread then resumes naturally with "People also differ in how well their confidence tracks their accuracy".

- **Line 64.** "Expected utility theory says to multiply each outcome's utility by its probability and add. Offered a coin flip that wins 110 dollars ... an expected-value maximizer accepts". The paragraph shifts from expected utility to expected value without saying that they differ, and a careful reader will notice that a chooser with concave utility could refuse the flip without violating expected utility theory. Either add one clause, "the simplest version of the theory, in which utility is just money, says to accept", or recast the example so that it follows from expected value only: "The simplest rule, expected value, says to multiply each payoff by its probability and add. Offered a coin flip ... a chooser following that rule accepts ... most people refuse, and they go on refusing at stakes so small that no reasonable curve of utility for wealth can explain it."

- **Line 68 (dangling modifier).** "Described as saving lives, 72 percent chose the program ... described as losing lives, 78 percent chose the gamble". It is the programs that were described, not the 72 percent. Rewrite: "When the programs were described in terms of lives saved, 72 percent chose the one that saved 200 for certain over a gamble with the same expected outcome; when they were described in terms of lives lost, 78 percent chose the gamble over the certain death of 400, though the outcomes were identical." Also, "an outbreak was expected to kill 600 people" has no disease. Use "an unusual disease was expected to kill 600 people", which is Tversky and Kahneman's own framing.

- **Line 70.** "The emotional circuits of Chapter 7 appear here directly." The next sentence is about the ventral striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, which Chapter 7 treated as valuation regions, and the emotional circuit (the amygdala) does not appear until three sentences later. Rewrite: "The circuits of Chapter 7 appear here directly." Alternatively, move the sentence to open the De Martino, Camerer, and Adolphs paragraph after the split recommended above.

- **Line 72.** "most preferred the known urn, whichever color they bet on, which no assignment of probabilities to the unknown urn can justify." The logic is the point of the experiment and is left for the reader to reconstruct. Add: "Preferring the known urn for a bet on red implies that red is less likely than half in the unknown urn, and preferring it for a bet on black implies the same of black, and both cannot be true. The preference is now called ambiguity aversion." This also defines "ambiguity aversion" before line 72's last sentence uses it.

- **Line 86 ("bandit" is never defined).** The body says "a set of slot machines with fixed but unknown payoffs" (line 86) and then "a four-armed bandit" (line 90), and the Figure 8.6 caption has "a ten-armed bandit". Define the term at line 86: "For one simplified version, a row of slot machines with fixed but unknown payoffs, known after the casino's one-armed bandit as the multi-armed bandit problem, John Gittins showed ..." Also, "it has no general solution" overstates the case, since the Gittins index is a general solution for its class. Use "it has no solution that is both general and practical".

- **Line 90.** "People do both kinds of exploration." Two kinds have not been named. Line 86 describes only the uncertainty bonus, and random exploration appears only in the figure legend. Rewrite: "There are two ways to explore: at random, by now and then choosing something other than the apparent best, or by direction, choosing the option about which least is known. People do both." The later sentence "They used both directed exploration, aimed at uncertainty, and random exploration, which is cheaper" then becomes a confirmation rather than a first definition, and can shrink to "Their exploration was both directed and random."

- **Line 90.** "How much to explore should also depend on how fast the world is changing, and Timothy Behrens and colleagues found that people's learning rates rose". The finding is about how fast people learn, not how much they explore. Rewrite: "How fast to learn should depend on how fast the world is changing, and Timothy Behrens and colleagues found ..."

- **Line 96 (text and caption disagree on the source).** The text credits the extended-training result to "Christopher Adams and Anthony Dickinson"; the Figure 8.7 caption says "after Adams 1982"; footnote 52 cites both. The 1981 paper with Dickinson established devaluation, and the 1982 paper by Adams alone compared moderate and extended training. Rewrite: "The question ... was given a sharp test by Christopher Adams and Anthony Dickinson, and sharpened by Adams alone a year later." Have the expert reviewer confirm the attribution. Also add why the test was run without pellets, which is the clever part of the design: "and the rats were tested on the lever with no pellets delivered, so that they could not learn the new value of the sugar from the lever itself".

- **Line 102.** "In a two-step task designed to separate the systems" The task is not described, and "two-step" tells the reader nothing. Add one sentence: "Each choice led, usually but not always, to one of two further choices, and only a learner with a model of those transitions would treat a reward reached by the rare route differently from one reached by the common route." Also, "more than a thousand people" should be "more than 1,000 people" (numerals above ten, as Chapter 7's review applied to "more than 200").

- **Line 104 (appositive inside commas).** "Pavlovian responses, approach to cues that predict reward and withdrawal from cues that predict harm, are emitted whether or not they serve the animal." This is the construction Chapter 7's review flagged four times. Rewrite: "Pavlovian responses (approach to cues that predict reward, withdrawal from cues that predict harm) are emitted whether or not they serve the animal." Also define "instrumental" at its first use in the next sentence ("can override instrumental learning, the learning of actions by their consequences, even when ...").

- **Line 110 ("defects ... features").** "Loss aversion, ambiguity aversion, steep discounting, and habit are often treated as defects of a rational machine. On the account developed here they are features of a machine that must decide quickly". This is the "not a bug, a feature" formula the house style asks writers to avoid. It also states as settled a view that the chapter itself qualifies at line 70 (Gal and Rucker). Rewrite: "Loss aversion, ambiguity aversion, steep discounting, and habit are often treated as defects of a rational machine. Many of them make better sense as the policies of a machine that must decide quickly, with limited computation, in a world that is uncertain, changing, and sometimes adversarial." Keep the last sentence as it stands.

- **Line 120.** "The account explains why drug-related habits are so hard to break and is one of several, and with Gillan's compulsivity dimension it is part of a broader program, computational psychiatry, that tries to describe disorders ..." Three ideas are joined by "and", and "is one of several" arrives too late to qualify "explains". Rewrite: "It is one of several accounts of addiction, and it explains why drug-related habits are so hard to break. Together with Gillan's compulsivity dimension, it belongs to a broader program, computational psychiatry, that tries to describe disorders as specific failures of the machinery this chapter has described." Also add the callback that Chapter 7 earned: after "grows without limit", add "Chapter 7 called addiction a form of reward hacking; this is how the hack works."

- **Line 124 (one-sentence paragraph).** "Decision-making under uncertainty is the field where neuroscience and artificial intelligence have exchanged the most, because reinforcement learning is a theory of both." Chapter 7's AI section opened with a two-sentence frame. Add a second sentence that sets up the five points: "The comparison is therefore less about what machines lack than about which of the brain's solutions they have already borrowed and which they have not."

- **Line 130.** "People adjust exploration to the horizon and to the volatility of the world, and a neuromodulatory system appears to set the rate." "A neuromodulatory system" is vague after Chapter 3 has named it. Use "and norepinephrine, on the account of Chapter 3, appears to help set the rate". The point also has no citation, unlike points two and four; consider citing an AI method that adapts exploration (for example, count-based or curiosity-driven exploration bonuses) if the expert reviewer agrees.

- **Line 132.** "AlphaGo ... paired networks trained to propose good moves at a glance and to judge positions with a search that simulated future play". The syntax suggests the networks judged positions with a search. Rewrite: "AlphaGo, the first program to beat a professional Go player, paired two networks, one trained to propose good moves at a glance and one to judge positions, with a search that simulated future play from a model of the game."

- **Line 6 (epigraph not paid off).** "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know." The quotation is authentic and the edition is recorded. But nothing in the chapter returns to it, whereas Chapter 7 paid off its James epigraph in the body. As it stands it reads as a Chapter 7 epigraph, since it is about feeling against reason. Pascal is also, with Fermat, the inventor of expected value, and his wager is the first decision-theoretic argument, so the payoff is easy. At line 22, add: "The idea of weighting outcomes by their probabilities goes back to Blaise Pascal, whose correspondence with Pierre de Fermat in 1654 founded the theory of probability, and whose wager on the existence of God is the first argument in decision theory. The same Pascal wrote that the heart has its reasons, and this chapter is about both halves of him." Have the expert reviewer verify the date, or drop it.

- **Line 24.** "These are the regions whose damage, in Chapter 7, left choice impaired". Chapter 7 named the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and this paragraph names the orbitofrontal and medial orbitofrontal cortex. A lay reader will not know that these overlap. Use: "The medial orbitofrontal cortex is part of the ventromedial prefrontal region whose damage, in Chapter 7, left choice impaired, and patients with such damage ..."

- **Line 28 (appositive chain).** "showed that this is divisive normalization, a computation that sensory circuits use to keep responses within range by dividing each one by the pooled activity of its neighbors, a cousin of the lateral inhibition of Chapter 4, here applied to value." The sentence carries three trailing appositives. Rewrite: "showed that this is divisive normalization, a computation that sensory circuits use to keep responses within range by dividing each one by the pooled activity of its neighbors. It is a cousin of the lateral inhibition of Chapter 4, here applied to value." Also make the decoy sentence concrete, since it is the reader's way in: "It helps explain why adding a decoy, such as a third, plainly worse option, can change what people choose between the original two."

## Length and balance

The body is about 5,200 words, against about 6,700 for Chapter 7 and the house range of 4,000 to 5,000 set for the early chapters. Length is not the problem; distribution is. The first half (Problem through Price of Waiting, about 3,100 words) is dense and well paced. "Accumulating Evidence" (810 words) is the longest section and earns it, because it carries the chapter's central mechanism, its best history, and its strongest figure; I would not cut it. "Risk, Loss, and Ambiguity" (520 words) and "Goals and Habits" (530 words) are also proportionate.

The second half shrinks to a run of sketches, and four sections are thin:

- **"Knowing How Sure You Are" (205 words): expand by about 120 words.** It has one experiment on confidence (Kiani and Shadlen) and then changes the subject. Add one sentence on what confidence is for, using a vivid animal result. Adam Kepecs and colleagues trained rats to make an odor judgment and then wait for an uncertain reward, and the time a rat was willing to wait tracked the probability that it had been right (Kepecs et al. 2008, *Nature*; Lak et al. 2014, *Neuron*, for the orbitofrontal requirement; have the expert reviewer verify both). This gives the section a second creature and makes the AI point at line 128 land harder.
- **"Thinking Is Costly" (220 words): expand by about 120 words** with the recognition-heuristic experiment (see Major) in place of one of the four "proposed" sentences. The section's claim, that simple rules can beat elaborate ones, needs one case the reader can picture.
- **"Choosing Among Others" (190 words, one paragraph): expand to two paragraphs, about 150 words more.** Either develop "learned in a culture" (Henrich's societies, already cited, can carry one more concrete sentence: which societies offered most and why), or add one causal experiment to set beside Sanfey's correlation. Daria Knoch and colleagues disrupted the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex with transcranial magnetic stimulation, and their participants then accepted more unfair offers while still judging them unfair (Knoch et al. 2006, *Science*; verify). That result echoes the chapter's opening theme of knowing the rule but not acting on it. Alternatively, fold the section into "Risk, Loss, and Ambiguity" as a final paragraph on social uncertainty. As a standalone section of one paragraph it looks unfinished.
- **"When Choice Goes Wrong" (210 words): expand by about 60 words** with the Chapter 7 callback and the closing sentence that hands over to Chapter 9 (see Major and Minor above).

The cuts that offset these additions are small: the retelling of Fellows and Farah at line 34 (about 30 words), and the restated definition of directed and random exploration at line 90 (about 15 words). The result would be a body of about 5,600 to 5,700 words, with the second half carrying its share of experiments. That is still shorter than Chapter 7 and appropriate for the material. No section is overloaded enough to need cutting beyond these.
