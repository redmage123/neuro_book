# Literary Review, Round 2: Chapter 7, "Emotion and the Body: How Internal States Tell the Brain What Matters"

Source: `docs/book/Word/Chapter7/How_the_Brain_Learns_Chapter_7_Emotion_and_the_Body.md` as revised (body lines 10 to 194; about 6,630 words of prose body by my count, excluding headings, captions, epigraph and footnote text; 7 figures; 107 footnotes). Line numbers below refer to the revised file.

## Overall assessment

The revision fixed what mattered. The thesis (line 16) is now framed as an argument "which most of the competing theories of emotion can accept", and line 76 closes the loop by saying what every position agrees on. "When Regulation Goes Wrong" now follows allostatic load. "Feeling and Choosing" ends the body with a real handover: "How those signals are combined, weighed against uncertainty, and turned into a decision is the subject of Chapter 8" (line 174). "What an Emotion Is" no longer stalls. The expert's additions are mostly well absorbed, and two of them improve the chapter. The first is McGaugh's vagal relay, which ties back to Figure 7.2: "Adrenaline does not enter the brain; McGaugh's laboratory showed that its effect is relayed by the vagus nerve, the interoceptive pathway of Figure 7.2" (line 148). The second is the Cahill caveat, which ends: "this study cannot separate encoding from consolidation; the animal work does that" (line 150). The Iowa section is now honest without becoming dull: "The interpretation has not held up well" (line 172) is followed by three crisp counter-findings. The 74-millisecond recording (line 98) balances the low-road debate where before it only had a rebuttal. Length is now about 6,630 words, close enough to the target that I would not cut further.

What keeps the chapter from 5/5 on prose is a set of small seams left by compression and insertion. There is one surname that now appears without its introduction, three lost transitions, a garden-path sentence, a double colon, a tense slip, two qualifiers that hint at a point the text never makes, a parenthetical gloss that sits awkwardly, and one paragraph (lines 124 to 130) where two inserted findings do not quite fit the sentence that frames them. Gage's new quotation also changes what the Gage story proves, and the next paragraph has not caught up. None of these is structural, and all can be fixed in an hour. Prose quality: 4/5 (5/5 once the findings below are fixed). Reader interest: 5/5.

## Findings

### Major

None.

### Minor (stand between this chapter and 5/5 on prose)

- **Line 64 (surname without introduction, new).** "In people, Garfinkel and colleagues found that fearful faces flashed..." The earlier sentence that introduced Sarah Garfinkel by full name (old line 50) was cut at line 52, so this is now her first mention. Rewrite: "In people, Sarah Garfinkel and colleagues found..."
- **Lines 160 to 164 (Gage no longer fits the frame, new).** Harlow's newly quoted words, that Gage had become "a child in his intellectual capacity" (line 162), together with "much of what is told about him is embellished", mean Gage no longer illustrates reasoning spared and choice impaired. Yet the paragraph on Elliot (line 164) opens as if continuing the same case. Add the contrast: "Antonio Damasio described a cleaner modern case, a patient he called Elliot, who after surgery for a tumor in the same region..." At line 164 also change "Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis explains such cases" to "Damasio proposed the somatic marker hypothesis to explain such cases". "Explains" asserts what line 172 then says "has not held up well".
- **Line 162 (mixed list).** "Earlier reports had described a good recovery, he later drove coaches in Chile, and much of what is told about him is embellished." This yokes a source, a biographical fact and a judgment into one series. Rewrite: "Earlier reports had described a good recovery, and in the years before Harlow wrote, Gage drove stagecoaches in Chile; much of what is told about him is embellishment."
- **Lines 124 to 130 (inserted findings do not fit their frame).**
  - Line 124 opens "The settings of this system are themselves learned" and closes "The stress system's predictions, like perception's priors, are written by experience". Between those sentences it puts the hand-holding study, which shows an immediate effect of company rather than a learned setting. "The settings are also shaped by company" therefore misdescribes it. Rewrite the middle sentence: "Its output also depends on company in the moment: women in an MRI scanner who were threatened with a mild shock showed less activity in threat-related regions while holding their husband's hand." Then move the closing sentence about experience up to follow the maternal-care sentence.
  - In the same paragraph, "glucocorticoid receptors" is undefined. Use "more of the receptors through which cortisol acts in the hippocampus".
  - At line 130 the cortisol sentence ("Cortisol has an inverted U of its own...") was appended after what used to be the paragraph's closing line ("the right trade in an emergency and the wrong one in an examination"), and Figure 7.6, which shows norepinephrine and dopamine, follows it. Move the cortisol sentence up to follow Arnsten's mechanism sentence. Then write "Figure 7.6 shows the shape for norepinephrine and dopamine" and end the paragraph on the examination line.
- **Line 112 (unexplained qualifier, new).** "In adults, the prefrontal cortex mostly inhibits the expression of the amygdala's memory rather than erasing it" "In adults" implies a contrast with the young that the text never draws. Either add it ("In infant rats extinction appears to erase the fear memory; in adults, the prefrontal cortex mostly inhibits...") or drop "In adults". In the same paragraph, "In people the corresponding area is the ventromedial prefrontal cortex.[^58]" has become a stub that no longer says what was found. Use "In people the corresponding area, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, is engaged when an extinguished fear is recalled the next day.[^58]"
- **Line 62 (parenthetical gloss).** "(Adrenaline, which American physiologists call epinephrine, is the hormone of the adrenal gland and a close chemical relative of the norepinephrine of Chapter 3.)" A whole sentence in parentheses after the paragraph's point reads like a footnote that escaped. Put it at first use: "...and an injection of adrenaline, the adrenal hormone that American physiologists call epinephrine and a close chemical relative of Chapter 3's norepinephrine, makes people feel as if they were afraid without making them afraid." In the same sentence, "the same visceral changes accompany different emotions, and fever and exercise as well" is still a near-fragment. Use "...accompany different emotions, and states that are not emotions at all, such as fever and exercise".
- **Line 66 (ambiguous "weaker").** "The human tests that remove or impose bodily signals are weaker." This could mean weaker evidence or weaker effects. Rewrite: "Tests in people that remove or impose bodily signals have given smaller and less consistent effects."
- **Line 68 (lost transition).** The Schachter and Singer paragraph lost its topic sentence ("If the body supplies arousal, something else must say what the arousal is about") and now opens cold on "In 1962". Restore it, shortened: "If the body supplies arousal, something else must say what it is about. In 1962..."
- **Line 30 (undefined term, new).** "the baroreflex detects the fall in blood pressure" The term is not defined. Use "a reflex driven by pressure sensors in the great arteries, the baroreflex, detects the fall..."
- **Line 26 (vestigial appositive).** "Bard showed that the display, hissing and racing heart included, disappeared only when the posterior hypothalamus was removed as well." The description of the display was cut from the preceding sentence, so "hissing and racing heart included" now interrupts for no reason. Move it to the Cannon and Britton sentence ("flew into fits of rage, hissing with racing hearts, at the slightest touch") or delete it.
- **Line 122.** "cortisol's actions, which depend on timing, include restraining the stress response itself" "Which depend on timing" is opaque here. Rewrite: "Robert Sapolsky and colleagues showed that some of cortisol's actions prepare the body for demand while others, arriving later, restrain the stress response itself before it does damage."
- **Line 134 (tense slip).** "much of the hippocampal shrinkage reverses when the stress ends, but the growth in the amygdala, and the anxious behavior that goes with it, persisted three weeks into recovery." Rewrite: "...but the growth in the amygdala, and the anxious behavior that goes with it, persists at least three weeks into recovery."
- **Line 138 (double colon).** "Donald Klein proposed in 1993 that spontaneous panic attacks are false suffocation alarms: a suffocation monitor, set too sensitive, signals..." This follows "Panic disorder is the clearest case:". Rewrite: "Panic disorder is the clearest case. Donald Klein proposed in 1993 that spontaneous panic attacks are false suffocation alarms: a suffocation monitor, set too sensitive, signals..."
- **Line 140 (unclear antecedent and stub).** "Its overlap with inflammation-induced sickness behavior is one reason the proposal is taken seriously." Here "Its" could refer to allostasis, the brain, or the proposal. Use "Depression's overlap with the sickness behavior that inflammation induces is one reason...". The next sentence, "Alexithymia, a difficulty in identifying one's own feelings, goes with poorer interoception.", is a stub. Join it: "And alexithymia, a difficulty in identifying one's own feelings, has been linked with poorer interoception across many kinds of bodily signal."
- **Line 154 (garden path).** "Students asked by Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch the morning after the Challenger shuttle exploded in 1986 gave, two and a half years later, accounts that often differed from their originals and were held with high confidence." The main verb arrives 25 words in. Rewrite: "The morning after the Challenger shuttle exploded in 1986, Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch asked students how they had heard the news. Two and a half years later, many gave different accounts and held them with high confidence."

### Nit (optional)

- **Line 156.** "Emotion tells the brain to keep a memory and to trust it..." This has become a one-sentence paragraph. Join it to line 154 as that paragraph's last sentence.
- **Line 148.** "Given after the training, it could not have changed what the rats experienced, only what they kept." "It" has to reach back past "doses" and "too little". Use "Given after the training, the injection could not have changed...".
- **Line 104.** "detected by chemical sensors elsewhere, presumably in the brainstem" Use "detected by chemical sensors outside the amygdala, probably in the brainstem".
- **Line 128.** "and the reading of their curve as a law of arousal came decades later." This is a good caveat but a flat end. Use "...and it was later writers who turned their curve into a law of arousal."
- **Line 40.** The Siegel paragraph lost its human sentence. One clause would restore the stakes: "...and without them the body is unprepared, which is thought to contribute to overdoses in people who take a familiar dose in an unfamiliar place."
