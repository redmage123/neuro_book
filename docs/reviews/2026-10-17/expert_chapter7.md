# Expert review: Chapter 7, Emotion and the Body: How Internal States Tell the Brain What Matters

File: `docs/book/Word/Chapter7/How_the_Brain_Learns_Chapter_7_Emotion_and_the_Body.md`

## Overall assessment

Technical accuracy score: **4 / 5**

This is the most carefully hedged chapter in the book so far. Most of the experiments are described accurately, and I checked the numbers against the abstracts or the original tables: Siegel et al. 1982 (96.4, 32.4 and 64.3 percent mortality); Lin et al. 2011 (optogenetic stimulation of VMHvl makes males attack females and inanimate objects); Chen, Lin, Kuo and Knight 2015 (AgRP activity reversed by the sight or smell of food before eating, scaled by palatability and nutritional state); Betley et al. 2015 (negative-valence teaching signal); Critchley et al. 2004; Garfinkel et al. 2014 and 2015; Hsueh et al. 2023; Feinstein et al. 2011 and 2013 (3 of 3 patients and 3 of 12 controls panicked); Romanski and LeDoux 1992; Paton et al. 2006; Milad and Quirk 2002; Vyas et al. 2002; Magariños and McEwen 1995; Dum, Levinthal and Strick 2019; Cahill et al. 1994; Talarico and Rubin 2003; Bechara et al. 1994 and 1997 (the deck payoffs are right); Maia and McClelland 2004; Kandasamy et al. 2016 (18 traders); Dutton and Aron 1974 (9 of 18 versus 2 of 16).

The contested positions are presented fairly. On James-Lange, Cannon's five objections are stated correctly and then qualified. Basic and constructed emotion are given equal weight, and the Siegel 2018 and Kreibig 2010 meta-analyses are cited for both sides. The low road is called "unsettled", allostasis "is argued", and the psychiatric accounts are called "hypotheses ... largely correlational". The anatomy of the interoceptive pathways, the threat circuit and the HPA axis is correct at the level of a popular text.

The problems are:
- one physiological claim that is wrong: standing up is handled mainly by the reactive baroreflex, so the brain does largely "wait";
- a primary source misdescribed (Harlow on Gage's intellect);
- the somatic marker evidence is presented more favorably than the cited critical review warrants, and two directly relevant tests are omitted (Heims et al. 2004 on pure autonomic failure; Fellows and Farah 2005 on reversal learning);
- an unsupported claim about what dopamine neurons signal;
- beta blockers listed among "treatments that work";
- the Yerkes-Dodson history, which is the usual retrofit.

All cross-references to Chapters 1, 3, 5 and 6 match what those chapters say (details at the end).

## Major (factually wrong, must fix)

- **Line 30**: "If you stand up quickly, gravity pulls about half a liter of blood into your legs, and if the brain waited for the fall in blood pressure to reach the brainstem before tightening the vessels, you would faint every time."
  - **What is wrong**: This is the opposite of the established physiology. The main defense against orthostatic pooling is the arterial baroreflex, which is a classic reactive negative-feedback loop. Carotid and aortic baroreceptors detect the fall in pressure, the nucleus of the solitary tract relays it, and vagal withdrawal and sympathetic vasoconstriction follow within one or two heartbeats. The brain does wait for the error, and people do not faint, because the loop is fast. Healthy people commonly show a transient pressure drop in the first 10 to 15 seconds of active standing (initial orthostatic hypotension; Wieling et al. 2007, Clin. Sci. 112:157), which shows that the regulation is not anticipatory enough to prevent the error. Feedforward contributions exist (central command, the vestibulosympathetic reflex), but they are secondary.
  - **Evidence**: No citation supports the sentence. The Krogh and Lindhard citation that follows is about exercise, where the anticipatory claim is correct.
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "If you stand up quickly, gravity pulls about half a liter of blood into your legs and blood pressure dips; sensors in the arteries detect the drop and the brainstem tightens the vessels within a heartbeat or two, fast enough that most people only feel a moment's light-headedness. Exercise shows what the brain does when it can see the demand coming." Alternatively, keep the sentence but use the vestibular feedforward signal as the example, citing Yates and colleagues, and say it supplements the baroreflex rather than replacing it.

## Moderate (misleading or outdated, should fix)

- **Line 134**: "his physician, John Harlow, reported that his intellect seemed intact while his character had changed".
  - **What is wrong**: Harlow (1868) said the reverse about intellect: "The equilibrium or balance, so to speak, between his intellectual faculties and animal propensities, seems to have been destroyed ... A child in his intellectual capacity and manifestations, he has the animal passions of a strong man." "Intellect intact" is Damasio's later gloss, not Harlow's report. Harlow's own 1848 note and Bigelow's 1850 report described Gage as largely recovered, and the personality account dates from 1868 (Macmillan 2000, *An Odd Kind of Fame*, MIT Press).
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "his physician, John Harlow, wrote twenty years later that the balance between his intellectual faculties and his animal propensities seemed to have been destroyed, and that his friends said he was 'no longer Gage' ..." Cite Macmillan for "the records are thin".

- **Lines 136 to 140 (somatic markers)**: "Barnaby Dunn and colleagues reviewed the wider evidence and found it consistent with, but not decisive for, the somatic marker account." Also: "Whether bodily feelings are necessary for good choices or merely accompany the value signals that are, they are part of the machinery."
  - **The Dunn review is more critical than stated**. Its abstract says the IGT findings are "undermined by the cognitive penetrability of the reward/punishment schedule, ambiguity surrounding interpretation of the psychophysiological data, and a shortage of causal evidence linking peripheral feedback to IGT performance", and it concludes that the hypothesis "requires additional empirical support to remain tenable" (PubMed 16197997).
  - **Two direct tests are missing**. Patients with pure autonomic failure, who have essentially no peripheral autonomic feedback, perform the Iowa gambling task normally (Heims, Critchley, Dolan, Mathias and Cipolotti 2004, Neuropsychologia 42:1979, PubMed 15381028). That is the most direct evidence on "necessary". The vmPFC patients' IGT deficit is largely explained by impaired reversal learning, and it shrinks when the initial run of large rewards from the bad decks is removed (Fellows and Farah 2005, Cereb. Cortex 15:58, PubMed 15217900).
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "... Barnaby Dunn and colleagues concluded that the hypothesis needs more evidence to remain tenable. Patients whose autonomic nerves have degenerated, so that their brains receive little feedback from the viscera, play the Iowa gambling task normally, and much of the ventromedial patients' difficulty is a failure to reverse an early preference. What survives is ... Whether bodily feelings contribute to good choices or merely accompany the value signals that drive them is open; the evidence that they are necessary is weak."

- **Line 78**: "much of the unexpected reward that a large population of dopamine neurons signals is unexpected relief of a need."
  - **What is wrong**: There is no citation and the claim is not established. Dopamine responses are modulated by satiety and need state, and that point is supported (for example Papageorgiou et al. 2016, Cell Rep. 15:1035, "Mesolimbic dopamine encodes prediction errors in a state-dependent manner"; Cone et al. 2016). But reward prediction errors are routinely recorded to rewards that relieve no deficit, such as juice in mildly restricted animals, sucrose in sated rats, money, and information. "Much of ... is relief of a need" is a quantitative claim nobody has measured.
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "and the size of the dopamine response to the same reward falls as the need it serves is met."

- **Line 148**: "the treatments that work, from exposure therapy to beta blockers to the reappraisal practiced in cognitive behavioral therapy".
  - **What is wrong**: Exposure therapy and CBT have strong evidence. Beta blockers do not: a systematic review found that "the quality of evidence for the efficacy of propranolol at present is insufficient to support the routine use of propranolol in the treatment of any of the anxiety disorders", and found no effect on PTSD through reconsolidation blockade (Steenen et al. 2016, J. Psychopharmacol. 30:128, PubMed 26487439). Their use is off-label, for the somatic symptoms of situational performance anxiety.
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "the treatments that work, such as exposure therapy and the reappraisal practiced in cognitive behavioral therapy, and the beta blockers some performers take to quiet a pounding heart, can be read as ways of changing the predictions or the signals they are built from."

- **Line 114**: "The chemistry of stress acts on the brain as well as the body, and its effects follow an inverted U. In 1908 Robert Yerkes and John Dodson trained mice ... and found that for a hard discrimination a moderate shock produced faster learning than a weak one or a strong one."
  - **The study does not support the framing**. Yerkes and Dodson varied shock strength and task difficulty in a handful of dancing mice per group. They measured neither arousal nor stress chemistry. For the easy discrimination, learning improved monotonically with shock strength. Only the harder discriminations showed an intermediate optimum, and it shifted toward weaker shocks as difficulty rose. The general "arousal inverted U" was attached to their paper decades later (Hebb 1955; Broadhurst 1957; see Teigen 1994, Theory Psychol. 4:525).
  - **The mechanism is not the 1908 result**. Arnsten's prefrontal inverted U is well supported in its own right and is the right mechanism to cite, but it is a separate finding.
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "In 1908 Robert Yerkes and John Dodson found that mice learned a hard discrimination fastest with a moderate shock for errors, though an easy one with a strong shock; decades later their curve was generalized, loosely, into the idea that performance peaks at moderate arousal. Amy Arnsten has found a mechanism for one version of that curve in the prefrontal cortex."

- **Line 26**: "Bard called it sham rage".
  - **What is wrong**: Cannon and Britton were already using "sham rage" in 1925 for decorticate cats (Am. J. Physiol. 72:283). Bard's 1928 contribution was to localize the critical tissue to the caudal hypothalamus. The rest of the paragraph is correct.
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "Cannon and his student Sydney Britton had called this sham rage ... Bard showed that it depended on the posterior hypothalamus."

- **Line 140**: "those who counted their heartbeats more accurately had made more money and lasted longer in the job".
  - **What is wrong**: The study was cross-sectional. Heartbeat detection correlated with relative profitability and with years already survived in the markets, measured at one time point (Kandasamy et al. 2016, PubMed 27641692). Nobody was followed forward, so "lasted longer" can be read as prediction. The current wording, with "suggestive rather than settled", is otherwise appropriate.
  - **Suggested rewrite**: "had made more money and had been trading for longer".

## Minor (imprecise wording)

- **Line 20**: Cannon coined "homeostasis" in 1926, in a contribution to the Richet festschrift, and elaborated it in the cited 1929 review. Use "in the 1920s", or "in 1926, and set it out fully in 1929".
- **Line 22**: "the brain, which burns glucose almost exclusively". This holds in the fed state. In prolonged fasting, ketone bodies supply up to about two thirds of brain energy, and lactate is also used. Add "in the fed state" or "mostly".
- **Line 46**: The lamina I to thalamus to posterior insula route (Craig's VMpo relay) is Craig's proposal, and the primate VMpo nucleus is disputed (Willis, Zhang, Honda and Giesler 2002, "A critical review of the role of the proposed VMpo nucleus in pain", J. Pain 3:79). The sentence already uses "Craig proposed", which is fine. Spinal visceral afferents also reach laminae V and X, not only lamina I.
- **Line 52 and Figure 7.2**: The text correctly says cytokines also reach the brain "through regions where the blood-brain barrier is leaky", but the figure routes "blood chemistry, immune signals" only through the vagus. Add a circumventricular-organ arrow (area postrema, subfornical organ) or recaption.
- **Line 54**: "turned down the pain signal at its source". Endogenous opioids released in placebo analgesia act centrally, through the periaqueductal gray to rostral ventromedial medulla descending pathway and at the dorsal horn, not at the tooth socket. Use "at the spinal cord" or "on its way to the brain". Also, Levine et al. was double-blind: patients knew they might receive a placebo, and "placebo responders" were those whose pain was "reduced or unchanged" (PubMed 80579). "Some reported less pain" is more accurate than "Many".
- **Line 62**: Hsueh et al. found the anxiogenic effect of pacing "only in risky contexts" (abstract). Adding that phrase strengthens the point that the body and the context together produce the state. The device was a wearable micro-LED harness with the opsin delivered systemically; "light shone through the chest" is acceptable.
- **Line 64**: "half of the men interviewed on the bridge called her". Strictly, 9 of the 18 men who accepted her number called her, against 2 of 16 on the control bridge. Use "half of the men who took her number".
- **Line 66**: "each with its own evolved circuit". Ekman's criteria are distinctive universal signals and physiology; dedicated subcortical circuits are Panksepp's version of the basic emotion view (Panksepp 1998, *Affective Neuroscience*). Use "evolved programs" or name Panksepp.
- **Line 70**: "What all three positions share is a coarse map ... along two dimensions". Basic emotion theorists accept valence and arousal as descriptive dimensions but deny that they are the fundamental ones. "a map that all three positions can accept as a description" is safer.
- **Line 80**: "Berridge and Robinson showed". The dissociation of liking from dopamine is shown; that dopamine encodes incentive salience rather than prediction error is still contested, which Chapter 3's dopamine caveats make relevant here. Consider "proposed, on this evidence".
- **Line 88**: The central nucleus influences the paraventricular nucleus, and so the stress hormones, largely indirectly, through the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis and other relays, because CeA output is GABAergic. Fine as a schematic. The figure's direct arrow should be read as "via relays".
- **Line 92**: For balance, human intracranial recordings show amygdala responses to fearful faces at about 74 ms, driven by low spatial frequencies, consistent with a fast subcortical route (Méndez-Bértolo et al. 2016, Nat. Neurosci. 19:1041). One clause citing it would make "unsettled" visibly two-sided.
- **Line 96**: "A threat from inside the body, detected by the brainstem's chemical sensors". Where the CO2 is detected is the authors' inference. The same group had shown that the amygdala itself is a CO2/pH chemosensor in mice (Ziemann et al. 2009, Cell 139:1012). Use "detected by chemical sensors elsewhere, presumably in the brainstem".
- **Line 102**: "The prefrontal cortex does not erase the amygdala's memory". This is the dominant view in adults. Extinction also produces some depotentiation in the lateral amygdala (Kim et al. 2007, PNAS 104:20955), and in very young rats it appears to erase (Gogolla et al. 2009, Science 325:1258). "does not, in adults, erase" is safer.
- **Line 112**: "Sapolsky and colleagues showed that cortisol's actions are mostly preparative and permissive". The review classifies the actions as permissive, suppressive, stimulatory and preparative, and argues that the mix depends on timing; "mostly" is not in the source. Also Strick: the abstract places the cognitive and affective networks "in regions of cingulate cortex", so drop "prefrontal" or use "cingulate and medial frontal".
- **Line 118**: Note that the stress-induced dendritic growth in the basolateral amygdala, unlike the CA3 atrophy, persisted after 21 days of recovery (Vyas, Pillai and Chattarji 2004, Neuroscience 128:667). This sharpens the chapter's point that fear is easily learned and rarely unlearned.
- **Line 124**: "the effect runs through the basolateral amygdala, where stress hormones release norepinephrine". Adrenaline does not cross the blood-brain barrier. Its memory effect is relayed by vagal afferents to the nucleus of the solitary tract and then to the amygdala (Williams and McGaugh 1993; McGaugh 2004, cited). This is an interoceptive pathway and a ready-made link to the vagus section at line 46. Suggested: "adrenaline, acting through the vagus nerve and the brainstem, releases norepinephrine in the basolateral amygdala".
- **Line 126**: In Cahill et al. 1994 the propranolol was given an hour before the slides, so it acted on encoding as well as consolidation. "acts as the third factor ... decides how strongly they are consolidated" therefore leans on the animal post-training work (line 124) more than on this study. A clause noting the timing would keep the logic tight.
- **Line 146**: Klein's 1993 hypothesis posits a "suffocation monitor" without fixing its location. "brainstem" is a reasonable gloss but is an addition.
- **Line 162**: Picard named the field in a 1995 MIT Media Lab technical report (No. 321); the book followed in 1997.
- **Figure 7.1 caption**: "overshoots and corrects late". The gray trace makes a large excursion but never crosses the set point. "swings far from the set point and corrects late" matches the plot.
- **Figure 7.3**: The positions are labeled approximate, which is acceptable. Compared with Russell's (1980) scaling, "tense" (about 92 degrees, near the top) and "happy" (about 8 degrees, near the pleasant pole) are placed noticeably lower-left and higher respectively. Moving them would make the figure closer to the source without changing its message.

## What an expert would expect and is missing (brief)

- **Human tests of James**: spinal cord injury studies. Hohmann (1966) reported reduced feeling; better-controlled later work (Chwalisz, Diener and Gallagher 1988, JPSP 54:820) found little reduction. Also facial feedback and its registered replication (Wagenmakers et al. 2016; Coles et al. 2019 meta-analysis: small effect).
- **Sustained anxiety versus phasic fear**: the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (Davis et al. 2010). This is relevant because LeDoux and Pine 2016 is cited.
- **Early-life programming of the HPA axis**: maternal care and glucocorticoid receptor expression (Meaney 2001). This is the clearest case of experience setting the stress system's predictions.
- **Glucocorticoid receptor types**: one sentence on mineralocorticoid versus glucocorticoid receptors, which underlie the inverted-U dose effects on memory.
- **Social regulation of physiology**: social buffering of the stress response, oxytocin. One line would do.
