# Literary Review, Round 2: Chapter 8, "Decision-Making Under Uncertainty: How the Brain Chooses When It Cannot Be Sure"

Source: `docs/book/Word/Chapter8/How_the_Brain_Learns_Chapter_8_Decision_Making.md` as revised (body lines 10 to 158; about 6,700 words of prose body excluding headings, captions, and footnote text). Line numbers refer to the current file.

## Overall assessment

Every round-1 Major has been implemented, and most of the fixes read as if they had always been there.

- **The new order works.** The reader goes from the dots teaser (line 14) straight to the dots (line 24). The value sections now build on accumulation instead of borrowing from it, and line 70's "the accumulation of the dots task, applied to worth rather than to motion" lands as a payoff.
- **The Pascal paragraph (line 20) is a real gain.** "This chapter is about both halves of him" pays off the epigraph in one stroke.
- **The Fellows and Farah payoff works (line 68).** It now explains the Iowa patients instead of retelling them.
- **The back third now has stories.** The odor-judging rats who wait in proportion to their confidence (line 48), the German students and San Antonio (line 124), and Knoch's stimulated responders "still judging the offers to be as unfair as before" (line 134) are each the kind of concrete case the chapter needed. The Knoch paragraph's tie to the Iowa patients is the best new sentence in the chapter.
- **The expert's additions improve the argument.** Rabin (line 74), the description-experience gap (line 84), Sozou (line 94), tonic dopamine and the cost of time (line 106), the LIP rebuttals (line 42), and the habits-without-values view (line 116) make the chapter more honest without hedging.
- **The ending works.** The Central Lesson answers the question line 12 asks, and both handovers to Chapter 9 work.

Reader interest is now 5/5.

The prose is not yet there. The additions were mostly appended to existing paragraphs, so five paragraphs now carry two or three topics each and run to 165 to 217 words (lines 40, 84, 94, 106, 134). Two of the additions also created seams: an antecedent lost at line 110 and an "account" that answers a different question at line 94. One round-1 nit fix has produced a sentence that contradicts itself (line 28), and one British spelling has crept in (line 120). All of these are small, mechanical fixes. Prose quality: 4/5. Reader interest: 5/5. With the items below fixed, the prose merits 5/5.

## Findings

### Major

None.

### Minor (stand between the chapter and 5/5 on prose)

- **Line 28 (self-contradictory sentence).** "and the eyes moved when the firing reached a common level, about 70 milliseconds before they began to move". The eyes cannot move 70 milliseconds before they begin to move. Rewrite: "and the firing reached a common level about 70 milliseconds before the eyes began to move, whatever the strength of the motion (Figure 8.2)."

- **Line 120 (British spelling).** "even when pecking it cancelled the food." House style is "canceled".

- **Line 110 (lost antecedent).** "Christopher Adams and Anthony Dickinson gave the question a sharp test in 1981, and Adams sharpened it a year later." The previous sentence (Tolman) states a claim, not a question, and "sharp ... sharpened" echoes. Rewrite: "Whether a given action is planned in this way or merely repeated was given a sharp test by Christopher Adams and Anthony Dickinson in 1981, which Adams refined a year later."

- **Line 94 (205 words, five accounts, one seam).** The paragraph runs Sozou, McClure, Kable and Glimcher, Hare, and serotonin together. "Todd Hare, Camerer, and Rangel offered a third account of self-control" follows two accounts of discounting, so "third" does not count anything the reader has been given. Split after the Sozou sentence, so that "Hyperbolic discounting is not obviously a mistake ... safe ones." stands as a short paragraph, or as the close of the Ainslie paragraph at line 90. Start the new paragraph at "How the brain computes it is debated." Then rewrite the Hare sentence: "A third view treats patience as self-control rather than as a discount rate. Todd Hare, Camerer, and Rangel found that in dieters choosing between tasty and healthy foods, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex did not compete with the valuation system but adjusted it, adding health to the value signal in those who chose well."

- **Line 106 (217 words, three topics).** The paragraph covers the marginal value theorem with the Shenhav dispute, then tonic dopamine and the cost of time, and then the foraging-template sentence, which now follows Parkinson's slowness rather than foraging. Split before "The average rate of reward also sets the cost of time itself." Move "Foraging may be the template ... looking for something better." to the end of the first paragraph, after the Shenhav clause, where it closes the foraging argument. The second paragraph (Niv) then ends the section on "move slowly", which leads well into the dopamine-based model-free system of "Goals and Habits".

- **Line 84 (three topics).** Gal and Rucker plus the Brown meta-analysis are one topic; the description-experience gap is another, and it deserves its own paragraph and its name. Split before "Prospect theory's overweighting of rare events" and name the effect: "... because a rare event is usually absent from a short run of experience. The difference has come to be called the description-experience gap."

- **Line 134 (Knoch and Henrich in one paragraph).** The paragraph moves from a disruption experiment to a cross-cultural survey without a turn, so the best line of the paragraph ("the Iowa patients' problem ... appearing in a social choice") is buried in the middle. Split before "Joseph Henrich and colleagues ran the game", so that Henrich and the closing sentence on fairness, body, and culture form the section's final paragraph.

- **Line 40 (187 words, two topics).** Bound-raising under conflict (theta and stimulation) and collapsing bounds and urgency (with the fixed-bounds caveat) are separate ideas. Split before "When the difficulty of the evidence varies from trial to trial". In the second paragraph, "How common this is remains debated" has a vague "this"; use "How often the brain uses collapsing bounds remains debated".

- **Line 126 (stub paragraph).** "Falk Lieder and Thomas Griffiths have proposed ... Shenhav, Matthew Botvinick, and Jonathan Cohen proposed ..." This is a two-sentence remnant of the old catalogue, with "proposed" twice. Join it to line 128 and give it a turn: "Falk Lieder and Thomas Griffiths have generalized the point into a framework, resource rationality, in which many apparent biases are optimal once the cost of computation is counted.[^80] The brain may even estimate that cost: Shenhav, Matthew Botvinick, and Jonathan Cohen argue that the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex weighs the expected value of exerting control and decides whether a situation is worth thinking hard about.[^81] This reframes the biases of the previous sections. ..."

- **Line 50.** "reversed in mid-movement, at rates that continued accumulation of the late evidence explained." The inverted relative clause forces a reread. Rewrite: "reversed in mid-movement, and continued accumulation of the late evidence explained how often they did so."

- **Line 140 (repetition).** "...specific failures of the machinery this chapter has described.[^91] In each case what fails is not reasoning but the machinery that learns what to choose". "Machinery" is used twice in consecutive sentences. Rewrite the second: "In each case what fails is not reasoning but the learning that decides what to choose, and the most persistent of those failures are habits that have outlived their reasons."

### Copyedits (optional; do not affect the score)

- **Line 32.** The final sentence chains a semicolon and two appositives ("Chapter 6's eye movements, each a decision about where evidence is most needed, are decisions of this kind about what to sample next"). Split it: "... carried out one sample at a time. Chapter 6's eye movements are decisions of the same kind, each a choice of where to sample next."
- **Line 104.** "or by direction, by choosing the option" Use "or in a directed way, by choosing the option" to avoid the double "by".
- **Line 116.** "Others argue that habits are not cached values at all". The house voice names people. Use "Kevin Miller, Amitai Shenhav, and Elliot Ludvig argue ...", which matches footnote 71.
- **Line 118.** The first long sentence ends on a 20-word "although" clause. Split it: "... that only a model could have supplied. Later analyses showed that some apparently model-based patterns can also be produced by sophisticated model-free strategies."
- **Line 96.** "a larger role for children's habits of waiting to eat" is vague. Use "a larger role for whether children were used to waiting before they ate", or whatever the source says more precisely.

Counts: Major 0, Minor 11, Copyedit 5.

## Length and balance

The body is about 6,700 words, up from about 5,200, so it is now level with Chapter 7. The distribution is sound. "Accumulating Evidence" (about 970 words) and "Risk, Loss, and Ambiguity" (about 770) are the longest sections and carry the chapter's central mechanism and its best-known findings. The formerly thin sections now run 270 to 300 words each, and each has at least one experiment the reader can picture. No section is overloaded in content; the density problem is at the paragraph level and is solved by the five splits above, which add no words. I do not recommend further expansion or cuts.
