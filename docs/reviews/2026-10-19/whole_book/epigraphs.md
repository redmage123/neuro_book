# Epigraph verification: Chapters 2, 3, 15 and frontispiece

Legend: PRIMARY = I read the passage in a scan or the original publication text. SECONDARY = only seen quoted by another author.

## 1. Chapter 2 epigraph: Cajal, "butterflies of the soul"

### English (Craigie and Cano translation)
- Wording in Chapter 2 (`Chapter2/...Neurons.md` line 6) matches the scholarly quotation exactly:
  "Like the entomologist in pursuit of brightly coloured butterflies, my attention hunted, in the flower garden of the gray matter, cells with delicate and elegant forms, the mysterious butterflies of the soul, the beating of whose wings may some day—who knows?—clarify the secret of mental life."
  - The translation uses the British "coloured" but the American "gray matter". "some day" is two words. "—who knows?—" is set with em dashes and no spaces.
- Source: SECONDARY. Hunter, DeFelipe, Mehta & Conway, "Art, Intuition, and Identity in Ramón y Cajal," *The Neuroscientist* (2025), doi:10.1177/10738584241234049 (PMC11707675). They quote it word for word and cite "Recollections of My Life (1989, p. 363)". A Current Biology dispatch (S0960982206026625) also cites the MIT edition at p. 363, according to the search index. I could not open that article (403).
- Primary not seen. The archive.org scan of the 1937 edition (item `bwb_C0-AVA-674`) is restricted, and search inside it is blocked. HathiTrust and Google Books were blocked or over quota.
- Verdict: the wording and page 363 are confirmed by two independent scholarly citations, but not from the book itself.
- Other English versions you may see, which are NOT Craigie: DeFelipe's OUP blog has "Like the entomologist hunting for brightly coloured butterflies..."; PMC11292737 has "...butterflies of striking hues...". Do not mix them.
- **Chapter 3 currently has an altered copy** of the same quote ("colored", "(who knows?)"). That is not the published translation. It goes away once Chapter 3 gets the new epigraph below.

### Spanish original: PRIMARY, two editions seen
- *Recuerdos de mi vida*, tomo II, *Historia de mi labor científica* (Madrid: Imprenta y Librería de Nicolás Moya, 1917), cap. VIII, p. 156 (archive.org `recuerdosdemivid02ramuoft`). The text spelling is "á" and "verjel":
  "¡Como el entomólogo á caza de mariposas de vistosos matices, mi atención perseguía, en el verjel de la substancia gris, células de formas delicadas y elegantes, las misteriosas mariposas del alma, cuyo batir de alas quién sabe si esclarecerá algún día el secreto de la vida mental!..."
- In the 3rd ed. (Madrid: Juan Pueyo, 1923; archive.org `BIUSante_ryc002`) the passage is in Part 2, cap. VII. It uses the modern spellings "a caza" and "vergel".
- PMC11292737 cites "Ramón y Cajal, 1917, pp. 155–156" for the same paragraph, which is consistent with what I found.
- The chapter covers the years 1890–1891: "Fueron los años de 1890 y 1891 períodos de intensa labor...". The passage comes from a section praising "el jardín de la neurología".
- **Correction needed in Chapter 3's old Epigraph note:** "from the chapter on his first work with the Golgi method" is inaccurate. The chapter covers 1890–91, his work on development, the olfactory bulb and the cerebral cortex. That note is replaced anyway (see item 3).
- Chapter 2's note, which says Recuerdos was "published in Spanish in 1901–1917", is fine: vol. I came out in 1901 and vol. II in 1917.
- Suggested addition to the Chapter 2 note, optional: "The Spanish original is in *Recuerdos de mi vida*, vol. 2, *Historia de mi labor científica* (Madrid: Moya, 1917), chap. 8, p. 156: 'las misteriosas mariposas del alma, cuyo batir de alas quién sabe si esclarecerá algún día el secreto de la vida mental.'"

## 2. Chapter 15 and frontispiece: "sculptor of his own brain"

### Spanish: PRIMARY, confirmed
- *Reglas y consejos sobre investigación científica (Los tónicos de la voluntad)*, 6th ed. (Madrid, 1923), "Prólogo de la segunda edición", p. x (archive.org `BIUSante_ryc012`). The preface is signed "Madrid, 20 de diciembre de 1898." The passage reads:
  "Consideremos, además, que todo hombre puede ser, si se lo propone, escultor de su propio cerebro, y que aun el peor dotado es susceptible, al modo de las tierras pobres, pero bien cultivadas y abonadas, de rendir copiosa mies."
- The same text appears in a modern edition (archive.org `ConsejosParaJovenesCientificos...`). There the heading is "Prólogo de la segunda edición (1899)", because the preface is dated Dec. 1898 and the edition came out in 1899. The underlying speech was read at the Real Academia de Ciencias on 5 December 1897, but the sentence is in the 2nd-edition preface, not the speech.
- Chapter 15's note line 158 quotes "todo hombre puede ser, si se lo propone, escultor de su propio cerebro." That is exact, and its dating ("preface to the second edition (dated Madrid, 20 December 1898)") is correct.

### Standard English (Swanson & Swanson, *Advice for a Young Investigator*, MIT Press, 1999)
- SECONDARY, consistent across Wikiquote, LibQuotes and the Goodreads/web index, all at p. xv:
  "...any man could, if he were so inclined, be the sculptor of his own brain, and that even the least gifted may, like the poorest land that has been well cultivated and fertilized, produce an abundant harvest."
  The usual stand-alone excerpt is "Any man could, if he were so inclined, be the sculptor of his own brain." The archive.org copy `adviceforyoungin0000ramo` is restricted, so I could not see p. xv myself.
- The book's wording, "Every man can, if he so desires, become the sculptor of his own brain.", is NOT Swanson's. Chapter 15's note correctly says "Translated for this book", so it is fine as an original translation. A more literal option would be "Every man can be, if he sets his mind to it, the sculptor of his own brain", since "puede ser" means "can be", not "become". The current rendering is a defensible paraphrase. "Every man if he so desires becomes sculptor of his own brain" also circulates (for example PMC3275903), so the book's version is close to a popular form.
- **Frontispiece** (`Frontispiece/...Front_Matter.md` line 8–10): it has the attribution "Santiago Ramón y Cajal" only. Consider adding "*Reglas y consejos sobre investigación científica* (1898)" for consistency with Chapter 15.

## 3. Chapter 3: replacement epigraph

Two constraints shaped the choice:
(a) Eve Marder's 2012 Neuron abstract ("the anatomical connectome provides a minimal structure and the neuromodulatory environment constructs and specifies the functional circuits that give rise to behavior") is the most apt, and I verified it word for word in PubMed and the PMC full text. But **Chapter 15 line 74 already quotes that exact sentence** in its body, so it should not be reused.
(b) Loewi's 1960 dream passage could only be verified through SECONDARY sources (Project MUSE blocks access behind a captcha), and those sources disagree on wording ("whether" vs. "whether or not"; "six o'clock" vs. "6.00 o'clock"; inserted years [1920] vs. [1921]).

### RECOMMENDED: Henry Dale, Nobel Lecture, 12 December 1936 (PRIMARY text seen; English original, not a translation)
These are the lecture's opening words, read in full on nobelprize.org (https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1936/dale/lecture/). The page reproduces the text from *Nobel Lectures, Physiology or Medicine 1922–1941* (Amsterdam: Elsevier, 1965):
"The transmission of the effects of nerve impulses, by the release of chemical agents, first became an experimental reality in 1921."
It is 21 words. It also bridges from Chapter 2, which already describes Loewi's 1921 experiment (Chapter 2 line 42, ref. Loewi 1921).
Page: the page number in the 1965 Elsevier volume could not be verified. The text says the quote is the lecture's first sentence, so the citation says "opening sentence" instead of giving a page.

Ready-to-paste top-of-chapter block:

> *"The transmission of the effects of nerve impulses, by the release of chemical agents, first became an experimental reality in 1921."*
>
> --- Henry Dale, *Nobel Lecture* (1936)

Ready-to-paste "## Epigraph" note (replaces the current Chapter 3 note at line 150–152):

## Epigraph

Henry H. Dale, "Some Recent Extensions of the Chemical Transmission of the Effects of Nerve Impulses," Nobel Lecture, Stockholm, 12 December 1936, opening sentence; reprinted in *Nobel Lectures, Physiology or Medicine 1922–1941* (Amsterdam: Elsevier, 1965), and online at nobelprize.org. Dale shared the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Otto Loewi, whose 1921 experiments on the frog heart (Chapter 2) first showed that a nerve acts on its target by releasing a chemical substance.

### Alternative A: Loewi, Nobel Lecture, 12 December 1936 (PRIMARY text seen, in the English translation printed on nobelprize.org; page unverified)
https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1936/loewi/lecture/ :
"In this way it was proved that the nerves do not act directly upon the heart, but rather that the direct result of nerve stimulation is the release of chemical substances and that it is these which bring directly about characteristic changes of function in the heart." (46 words, a translation from German, a bit clunky.)

### Alternative B: Loewi's dream (SECONDARY only; most evocative)
Text as given by todayinsci.com, which cites "An Autobiographic Sketch," *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine* 4 (1960), p. 17. The article runs pp. 3–25, doi:10.1353/pbm.1960.0006. Shortest apt excerpt (34 words):
"The next night, at three o'clock, the idea returned. It was the design of an experiment to determine whether the hypothesis of chemical transmission that I had uttered seventeen years ago was correct."
Caveat: the Singapore Med J 2014 (55:3) version reads "whether or not the hypothesis" and "3.00 o'clock". Check against a library copy of PBM 4(1), p. 17, before using. If used:

> *"The next night, at three o'clock, the idea returned. It was the design of an experiment to determine whether the hypothesis of chemical transmission that I had uttered seventeen years ago was correct."*
>
> --- Otto Loewi, "An Autobiographic Sketch" (1960)

Note: Otto Loewi, "An Autobiographic Sketch," *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine* 4, no. 1 (1960): 3–25, at p. 17, describing the night before Easter Sunday (secondary sources variously bracket the year as 1920 or 1921), when he woke with the design of the frog-heart experiment that established chemical neurotransmission.
