# Literary Review: Chapter 14, "Implications for Learning, Work, and Technology: What the Brain's Design Asks of the World We Have Built"

Source: `docs/book/Word/Chapter14/How_the_Brain_Learns_Chapter_14_Implications_for_Learning_Work_and_Technology.md` (body lines 10 to 140, about 6,120 words of prose by my count, excluding headings, captions, and notes). It has 7 figures and 68 footnotes.

## Overall assessment

What works:

- **The opening.** Kuhl's infants are the right opening for the applied chapter. "the same sounds, from the same people, were learned when they came from someone in the room and not when they came from a screen" (line 14) is a finding every parent and teacher will feel. The follow-up studies (line 16) refine it to "when someone on it, or beside it, responds" instead of letting the reader leave with a slogan.
- **The spine is honest.** The chapter asks "what the brain's design implies, and how strong the evidence is", and it begins with Bruer's "a bridge too far" (line 22) and the myths (lines 28 to 34). That order inoculates the reader before the advice begins.
- **The best passages:**
  - The Georgia governor's classical music for every newborn, set against Chabris's 1.4 IQ points (line 32).
  - The BBC's 11,430 brain trainers (line 34).
  - Kornell and Bjork's paintings, with "The difficulty that makes mixed study effective is the same difficulty that makes it feel ineffective" (line 48).
  - The Seattle start times (line 60).
  - Thamus, with the irony that his warning survives only in writing (line 84).
  - The London taxi drivers (line 88).
  - Bainbridge's ironies and Air France 447 (lines 92 to 94).
  - The Turkish students whose chatbot raised their practice scores and lowered their exam scores (line 100).
- **Contested claims are stated as contested,** without hedging adverbs: growth mindset, expressive writing, media multitasking, the phone on the desk, ego depletion, the parole boards, offloading, satellite navigation, exercise, and social media. For an applied chapter this is the chapter's greatest strength.
- **The AI section takes the chapter's own findings** and turns them into four principles that only this chapter could state.
- **The house style holds,** with no em dashes and no British spellings.

What does not work:

1. **The second half is written in catalogue paragraphs.** Lines 70, 88, 100, and 110 run 253 to 344 words and hold three to seven studies each. Line 110, on social media, is the longest paragraph in Part VI and has no scene at all. The chapter's most consequential question for many readers gets its least readable prose.
2. **The last sentence of the chapter misstates its first finding.** The Central Lesson ends: "The infants in Kuhl's laboratory learned from the person in the room, not from the same voice on a screen" (line 136). But line 16 established that what the screen lacked "was not a body but a response". The chapter's last image should carry its own refinement.

The smaller issues are these:

- **Figure numbering.** Figure 14.2 is cited and placed before Figure 14.1 (lines 14 to 24).
- **A misplaced paragraph.** The open-office study (line 78) is about attention and social withdrawal, not about how long a mind can work.
- **An idea raised and dropped.** Chapter 13's supernormal stimuli are invoked (line 108) and not used.
- **The summary figure.** Figure 14.7, the chapter's spine, is introduced in passing at the end of its shortest section.

Prose quality: 4/5. Reader interest: 4/5.

**To reach 5/5 on prose:** split the long paragraphs as below, fix the closing sentence, and make the Minor corrections.

**To reach 5/5 on reader interest:** give "Designed to Be Hard to Put Down" one experiment told as a scene, Allcott's paid Facebook deactivation, and let the other studies follow it. Tie the supernormal stimuli to the argument or let them go.

**On length:** the chapter is right for its job at about 6,100 words. An applied chapter that weighs evidence should be lean, and the reader is already carrying many numbers. I would not add more than the 100 to 150 words of the social-media scene. The work to do is restructuring, not expansion.

## Findings

### Major

- **Line 110 (344 words, seven studies, no scene).** Split it into three paragraphs and lead with an experiment the reader can picture.
  - **Paragraph one, the surveys.** Orben and Przybylski against Twenge, ending on the potatoes and glasses.
  - **Paragraph two, the experiments.** Open with Allcott told as a scene (about 60 words added; expert to confirm the figures): "Hunt Allcott and his colleagues found out how much people would have to be paid to give up Facebook for a month, a median of about $100, and then paid a randomly chosen group to do it, checking that their accounts stayed dark. The deactivated users gained about an hour a day ...". Then Hunt, then Braghieri.
  - **Paragraph three, the dispute and the verdict.** Haidt and Odgers, then "The fairest summary is ...". Introduce Haidt by his full name at his first mention: in the current text "Jean Twenge, Haidt, and their colleagues" comes before "Jonathan Haidt has argued".

- **Lines 70, 88, and 100 (254, 253, and 313 words).**
  - Line 70: split before "Interruptions at work carry a cost of a different kind".
  - Line 88: split before "Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot". The taxi drivers then stand alone as the section's story, and the satellite-navigation evidence and Spiers's explanation follow as their consequence.
  - Line 100: split before "Gregory Kestin and his colleagues" and again before "Measures of the brain itself are only beginning", so that Bastani, the chapter's central experiment for its AI argument, has a paragraph to itself. Then move "Figure 14.6 shows the result" to the end of the Bastani paragraph.

- **Line 136 (the closing sentence contradicts line 16).** Use "The infants in Kuhl's laboratory learned from a voice that answered them, and not from the same voice on a screen that could not." This also binds the ending to the chapter's thesis. The brain learns from the work it does and from people who respond to it, which is the principle the AI section's first point asks of tutors.

### Minor

- **Lines 14 to 24 (figure order).** The Kuhl figure is cited first (line 14) and placed first (line 18) but numbered 14.2, and the map follows as 14.1. Renumber the Kuhl figure 14.1 and the map 14.2, and update the citations at lines 14 and 22.
- **Line 22.** "Two warnings come first" announces two, but the second warning ("claims sold as 'brain-based' are often wrong") is a single sentence that is then interrupted by the figure introduction. Use "The second warning is that claims sold as 'brain-based' are often wrong, and the next section begins with them." Move the Figure 14.1 (map) sentence to its own sentence after it.
- **Line 32 (191 words).** Split before "The popular idea that people are 'left-brained'".
- **Line 40 (213 words) and line 42 (240 words).** Split line 40 before "Rohrer and his colleagues randomly assigned". Split line 42 before "Figure 14.3 shows why a gap can be too short or too long".
- **Line 48.** "Yet 78 percent of the participants did better with mixing, and 78 percent said that grouping had worked as well or better" puts the contrast word in the wrong place. Use "Seventy-eight percent of the participants did better with mixing, yet 78 percent said that grouping had worked as well or better."
- **Line 52.** "the circuit Chapter 3 described" covers the midbrain dopamine neurons, which are Chapter 3's, but not the hippocampus, which is Chapter 5's. Use "the dopamine signal of Chapter 3 and the hippocampus of Chapter 5".
- **Line 54 (211 words).** Split before "Another brief intervention shows how hard such effects are to pin down".
- **Lines 66 and 74.** Two consecutive sections open on "Chapter 8 described ...". Change line 74 to begin with the experiment: "Antonius Wiehler, Mathias Pessiglione, and their colleagues asked what a long day of effort does to the brain, the cost of thinking that Chapter 8 described, measured over hours."
- **Line 76.** "d = 0.04" is the only statistical notation in the chapter. Use "an effect size of 0.04, essentially zero".
- **Line 78 (misplaced).** The open-office study is about attention and withdrawal, and its last sentence already points back to "the limited attention the previous section described". Move it to the end of "Attention Is a Budget" (after line 70), and change that sentence to "perhaps to protect the limited attention this section has described".
- **Line 86.** "One widely reported study claimed more" is the only unnamed study in the section. Name it: "Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu, and Daniel Wegner claimed more ...".
- **Line 94.** The expert should confirm the time from the stall to impact. The investigators' report gives about three and a half minutes, which is "more than three minutes" rather than "more than four".
- **Line 108 (supernormal stimuli invoked and dropped).** Either cut "Chapter 13 described supernormal stimuli ..." or use it. One sentence at the end of the paragraph would do: "If Chapter 13's beetle is a guide, the risk is not that the brain's learning rules are wrong but that the inputs now offered to them are larger, faster, and more frequent than any they were tuned for."
- **Line 116 (placement of Figure 14.7).** The ledger summarizes the whole chapter but is introduced at the end of its shortest section. Introduce it in its own short paragraph after line 116, or move the sentence to the start of the AI section: "Figure 14.7 collects the chapter's verdicts on the evidence; four of them bear directly on how artificial intelligence should be built."
- **Line 130.** "a budget that Chapter 1 measured in watts" conflates attention with energy. Use "for a budget of attention that, like the brain's energy, cannot be enlarged on demand".
- **Line 136.** "in classrooms, offices, and in front of screens" should be "in classrooms, in offices, and in front of screens", as now in Chapter 13.

### Copyedits

- Line 12: "56.7 percent ... 65.7 percent" match the figure; good.
- Line 28: "about 89 percent" is fine as given.
- Line 92: the ellipsis inside the Bainbridge quotation is correct. Check that the PDF renders it as a single character.
- Figure 14.7: the green panel is the only green besides Figure 13.6 (now blue). Consider gray or a darker blue for "Supported, with caveats", to keep Part VI's palette. In the "Strong, including classroom trials" panel, "the cost of switching between tasks" rests on laboratory evidence, not classroom trials. Either retitle the panel "Strong" or move that item.
- Figure 14.3B: the annotation "5.2 days (75% of 7)" sits beside the text's "20 to 40 percent of a one-week delay" (Cepeda's data). The caption already says the model is flatter than the data. Adding "model" to the annotation ("model: 5.2 days") would prevent a reader from comparing the two numbers directly.

Counts: Major 3, Minor 15, Copyedit 5.

The balance is good. Ten sections of 420 to 610 words each carry one practical question, and "When the Machine Does the Work" (731) is the longest, as it should be, since it holds the chapter's newest and most consequential evidence. "Learning Across a Lifetime" (280) is short but complete. Moving the open-office paragraph shifts about 100 words from "How Long Can a Mind Work?" to "Attention Is a Budget". The Allcott scene adds about 60 words to "Designed to Be Hard to Put Down".

**Repetition with earlier chapters is well handled:**

- Chapter 5's forgetting curve and its three remedies (retrieval, spacing, sleep) are recalled in one sentence (line 38). The chapter then moves to classroom trials that Chapter 5 did not describe.
- Chapter 9's contextual interference is acknowledged, and Kornell and Bjork carry it into category learning.
- Chapter 13's supernormal stimuli are the one callback that is raised and not used (Minor, line 108).

With the proposed changes the body is about 6,200 words. I do not recommend expanding it further.
