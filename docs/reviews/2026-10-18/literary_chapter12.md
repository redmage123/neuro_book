# Literary Review: Chapter 12, "The Brain as a Predictive, Adaptive System: Is There One Principle Beneath the Mechanisms?"

Source: `docs/book/Word/Chapter12/How_the_Brain_Learns_Chapter_12_The_Brain_as_a_Predictive_Adaptive_System.md` (body lines 10 to 138, about 5,310 words of prose by my count, excluding headings, captions, and notes; the coordinator counts about 5,400). It has 7 figures and 50 footnotes.

## Overall assessment

What works:

- **The opening.** Craik is the right opening for Part VI. "A nervous system ... could carry a working model of the world ... run it forward to see what would happen, and act on the result before the world had a chance to deliver it" (line 12) states the chapter in a sentence. The bicycle on King's Parade the day before the war in Europe ended gives the idea a human cost. "much of what this book has described can be read as a footnote to it" is a bold and earned claim.
- **The thesis.** Line 20 is the clearest thesis in Part V or VI: prediction is a strategy, not a mechanism, and "The evidence for the strategy is strong. The evidence that one equation captures it is, so far, weak." The Central Lesson delivers exactly that.
- **The best passages:**
  - Laughlin's fly, whose response curve is the cumulative distribution of its world (line 38).
  - Addams at the Falls of Foyers, blaming his eyeball (line 46).
  - Keller's running mice, whose cortex fires at a halt that is "not a stimulus at all" (line 56).
  - "he spread the warm bread with socks" (line 58).
  - Powers's conditioned hallucinations (line 78).
  - Friston's own admission that the principle "cannot be falsified" (line 106).
  - Neurons in a dish playing Pong (line 108).
- **The free-energy section is fair.** It gives the attraction, the dark room, the testability problem, and the direct tests with their critics, so the reader can judge for themselves.
- **The house style holds.** There are no em dashes and no British spellings, and every figure is cited.

What does not work:

1. **Line 92 retells Chapter 6.** Chapter 6, line 42 already gives the light-from-above prior, its leftward bias, the bump and dent reversal, Adams, Graf, and Ernst's retraining, and the chicks reared under light from below. Chapter 12 repeats all five. This is the one place where the synthesis chapter simply reprints an earlier one.
2. **The chapter is short, and its middle is abstract.** At about 5,300 words it is 1,200 to 1,700 below recent chapters. The shortfall shows where no one is on the page:
   - "Why Predict?" argues from first principles, apart from the flash-lag effect.
   - "Many Errors, One Form" has the chapter's key idea, the learning rate set by uncertainty, but no scene.
   - "Adaptation on Every Timescale" is a list until it reaches the retold Chapter 6 paragraph.
   - "What Prediction Leaves Out" and the AI section have no experiment at all.
3. **Two paragraphs are catalogues.** Line 68 (290 words) runs Kalman, Nassar, the pupil study, Figure 12.5, Behrens, Yu and Dayan, and Mathys. Line 106 (278 words) runs five critiques and three empirical reviews.

The smaller issues are these:

- **Four callbacks are wrong.**
  - Chapter 6 has no aftereffects (line 44).
  - Synaptic scaling is in Chapter 5, not Chapter 2 (line 86).
  - Chapter 3 never names the "delta rule" (line 66).
  - Line 34 introduces Sterling as new, though Chapter 7 already credits him with allostasis.
- **The "three reasons" structure is broken.** The Duhamel paragraph (line 28) fits none of the three and sits between the first and second.
- **The handover is misplaced.** It is in the Central Lesson, not at the end of the AI section, where Chapters 9 to 11 put theirs.

Prose quality: 4/5. Reader interest: 4/5.

**To reach 5/5 on prose:**

- Replace the Chapter 6 retelling (line 92).
- Split and trim the two catalogue paragraphs (lines 68, 106).
- Fix the four callbacks and the three-reasons structure.
- Move the handover.

**To reach 5/5 on reader interest:** add four stories where the chapter is most abstract, as set out under "Length and balance":

- Cricket batsmen whose eyes jump to where the ball will bounce.
- Drivers in fog, and the slow-speed prior that training can move.
- Infants who bang the ball that passed through a wall.
- A fourth AI point on many errors against one loss.

Together these bring the body to about 6,000 to 6,200 words.

**On the closing sections:** the return to "Implications for Artificial Intelligence", "The Central Lesson", and "Next Chapter" is right for Part VI. The AI section's frame works well: "In one sense the brain's strategy has been vindicated at enormous scale. In three senses it has not yet been copied." Its second and third points sit close to Chapters 10 and 11, so the section needs one point that only this chapter can make.

**On the Next Chapter line:** it works. Line 120 ("questions about the constraints under which it was built, and they need answers of a different kind") sets it up exactly. "Why the Brain Works the Way It Does: Constraints, Trade-offs, and the Shape of a Solution" is long but clear, and I would keep it.

## Findings

### Major

- **Line 92 (retells Chapter 6, line 42).** Keep only the chick biological-motion result, which is new, and the closing sentence. Replace the rest with a prior the book has not met, which makes the same point with a different story (about 130 words; expert to verify all three studies): "The slowest prior can still be revised. People assume, other things being equal, that objects move slowly, which is usually true, and the assumption shows itself when the evidence weakens: a faint pattern looks slower than a clear one moving at the same speed. Robert Snowden and his colleagues found that drivers in a simulator, when the road was covered in fog, judged their speed to be lower than it was and drove faster to compensate. Alan Stocker and Eero Simoncelli showed that such misjudgments are what an observer with a prior for slow speeds would make, and Matthaios Sotiropoulos, Aaron Seitz, and Peggy Seriès trained people for a few days on fast, faint patterns and found that the prior itself had shifted toward faster speeds. Chapter 6's light from above, which touch could rotate and chicks reared under light from below still assumed, makes the same point for shape." Then continue with "Chicks hatched and kept in darkness ..." and end, as now, on "Evolution sets the prior; experience can move it, within limits."

- **Line 68 (290 words, seven sources).** Split before "Chapter 11 described Timothy Behrens's evidence". The first paragraph becomes Kalman, Nassar, the pupils, and the figure. The second becomes "who sets the rate": cut the Behrens clause to "As Chapter 11 described with Behrens's blue and green options, people adjust their learning rate to the volatility of the world." Keep Yu and Dayan and Mathys. If the expert can confirm it, give Nassar's task a picture: in the group's later version, people placed a bucket to catch bags dropped from a helicopter hidden behind clouds, which moved without warning. That one image makes the whole idea of an adaptive learning rate visible.

- **Line 106 (278 words, testability and evidence in one paragraph).**
  - Split before "What can be tested are the specific process theories built under it".
  - Trim the first half by removing the Bruineberg sentence or cutting it to a clause ("and Jelle Bruineberg and his colleagues showed that its central device, the Markov blanket, is used in two senses"), which saves about 40 words.
  - In the second half, the preprint sentence is the most interesting result. End the paragraph on it, as now, and keep Walsh and Aitchison and Lengyel as they are.

### Minor

- **Line 12.** "a short book" and "His book is short" repeat each other. Use "His book runs to barely 120 pages, and much of what this book has described can be read as a footnote to it" (expert to confirm the page count), or simply "The book is slight".
- **Line 12.** "He worked on it for two more years" has an unclear "it". Use "He spent the two years left to him on the problem, during the war, ..." The expert should also confirm the dates: whether Craik was struck on 7 May and died on 8 May.
- **Line 26 (story to add, after the flash-lag sentences; about 110 words; expert to verify Land and McLeod 2000).** "The clearest demonstrations come from sport. Michael Land and Peter McLeod recorded the eye movements of cricket batsmen facing fast bowling. The batsmen watched the ball leave the bowler's hand and then, without tracking it, jumped their eyes to the spot on the pitch where it would bounce, arriving there a fraction of a second before the ball did; the best of them made the jump earliest. They were not watching the ball so much as checking a prediction of it." This gives "Why Predict?" its human scene.
- **Line 28 (breaks the three reasons, and is close to Chapter 6's efference copy).** Tie it to delay: open with "Delay matters most for the brain's own movements, which change its input faster than the input can be processed." Or move it to "Prediction at Every Level" as a fourth example. Either way, the next paragraph's "The second reason" then follows the first.
- **Line 34.** Sterling's allostasis is in Chapter 7 (line 32 of that chapter). Use "and Peter Sterling, whose allostasis Chapter 7 described, has argued that ..."
- **Line 44 (callback).** "Adaptation of this kind, which Chapter 6 met in illusions and aftereffects" is wrong, since Chapter 6 discusses neither adaptation nor aftereffects. Cut the clause: "Adaptation of this kind is not fatigue." Addams, in the next paragraph, then introduces aftereffects properly.
- **Line 52 (205 words).** Split before "Figure 12.4 shows why".
- **Line 58.** "Forty years of work since" should be "More than 40 years of work since", since 1980 to the mid-2020s is about 45.
- **Line 66 (callback).** Chapter 3 never names the delta rule. Use "The simplest learning rule of this kind, the one beneath Chapter 3's temporal-difference learning, is called the delta rule: ..."
- **Line 86 (callback).** "Synaptic scaling, which Chapter 2 described" should be "which Chapter 5 described".
- **Line 98.** "a measurable upper bound on surprise" is wrong in one word: free energy is computable from the model, not measured. Use "a computable upper bound on surprise" (expert to confirm).
- **Line 116 (story to add; about 90 words; expert to verify Stahl and Feigenson, *Science* 2015).** Add after the first sentence: "Prediction error can tell a learner where to look, but not that it should want to. Aimee Stahl and Lisa Feigenson showed 11-month-old infants a ball that seemed to roll through a solid wall, or a toy car that seemed to float off a ledge. Handed the object afterward, the infants explored it more than one that had behaved as expected, and they tested the expectation it had broken: they banged the ball on the table and dropped the car. The surprise told them where to explore; the wish to explore was their own." This also makes "it has to be added" concrete.
- **Line 124 to 130 (AI section needs a point of its own).** Its second and third points restate Chapters 10 and 11. Add a fourth that only this chapter can make (about 80 words): "Fourth, the brain learns from many errors at once, of different kinds: a single number about reward broadcast widely, a pattern of errors in each sensory area, a movement error delivered to each Purkinje cell. A large model is trained on one loss, with a second for human preferences added at the end. Whether many local objectives, each fitted to its own part of the problem, would learn faster or more robustly than one global objective is a question the brain's arrangement poses directly." Change "In three senses" in line 124 to "In four senses".
- **Lines 128 and 134 (handover placement).** Move the last sentence of the Central Lesson ("What prediction leaves unexplained ... the subject of Chapter 13.") to the end of the AI section, where Chapters 9 to 11 put theirs. The Central Lesson then ends on its lesson: "... but the strategy does not depend on the equation."
- **Line 134.** "what the brain wants and why it is built as it is, depends on the constraints under which it evolved". This says Chapter 13 will explain "what the brain wants", which line 114 attributes to the body, not to constraints. When the sentence moves, use "What prediction leaves unexplained, why the brain is built as it is, depends on the constraints under which it evolved, and those are the subject of Chapter 13."

### Copyedits

- Line 108: "the remaining ninety" should be "the remaining 90" (house style, numerals above ten). Also "the first ten sessions" is fine.
- Line 128: "is old in machine learning, Richard Sutton proposed one version in 1992, but" is a comma splice. Use "is old in machine learning; Richard Sutton proposed one version in 1992. But it has not become ..."
- Line 92: "by about 26 degrees on average in one study" goes if the paragraph is replaced. If it is kept, name the study.
- Figure 12.5: the hidden mean (black) drops to about 35 on the last trial and spikes briefly near trial 30. If these are artifacts of the simulation's final step, trim the last trial; if they are real jumps, they are fine.
- Figure 12.1: the hippocampus row's error signal, "mismatch with the expected sequence", is the only row the chapter does not support with an experiment. Either cite one in the caption or soften it to "departure from the expected path".

Counts: Major 3, Minor 18, Copyedit 5.

**Does it need more?** Yes, but by stories, not argument. A synthesis chapter that ran to 7,000 words of argument would lose the clarity that is its strength. I recommend about 6,000 to 6,300 words, with the additions placed where the chapter is most abstract:

Cuts:

- Bruineberg trimmed (−40).
- Behrens compressed (−20).
- Line 44 clause (−10).

The result is about 5,650 words. To reach 6,000 to 6,300, two further stories would serve best:

- **A human case in "When the Learning Rate Goes Wrong" (about 120 words).** The voice-hearers of Powers's study include people without any diagnosis who attribute their voices to spiritual sources. One sentence on who they are and what they hear would make the section's point about a continuum, not a disorder, visible.
- **An opening story for "Adaptation on Every Timescale" (about 120 to 150 words)** that a reader will recognize, so that the list of timescales arrives with an experience behind it. Sea legs would do: sailors who still feel the deck rolling for hours or days after landing, a prediction outliving its world on a longer timescale than Addams's waterfall. The expert should supply a study.

With both, the body would be about 5,950 to 6,000 words. I would not go past 6,500.
